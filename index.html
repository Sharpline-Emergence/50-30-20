What Is the 50/30/20 Rule?

The 50/30/20 rule divides your after-tax income into three buckets: about half for needs, about 30% for wants, and about 20% for savings and paying down debt beyond the minimums. It comes from Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi's 2005 book All Your Worth, which grew out of Warren's research as a Harvard bankruptcy professor into why ordinary middle-class families end up broke.

The most important number is the 50. Her research found that families got into trouble when their must-pay costs climbed past roughly half of take-home pay, leaving no room to absorb a job loss, illness or rate increase. Keeping needs near 50% is what gives a household its margin for error. Read the full origin story →

Why Use Net Pay, Not Gross Salary?

Income tax, CPP and EI come off your cheque before you ever see it, so that money isn't available to spend or save. Running the split on gross salary produces targets you can't hit. If you're self-employed or drive for a gig app, nothing is withheld, so set aside money for the CRA first and use what's left.

What Counts as Needs, Wants and Savings?

The most common question is where specific expenses belong. Here's a breakdown for Canadian households, including the grey areas.

🏠 Needs — 50%

Bills you can't reasonably skip without serious consequences.

  • Rent or mortgage payment
  • Property tax and strata fees
  • Groceries (basic food)
  • Hydro, gas and water
  • Home or tenant insurance
  • Car insurance
  • Internet and a basic phone plan
  • Transit pass or fuel for work
  • Car payment (if needed for work)
  • Minimum debt payments
  • Prescriptions and dental not covered by a plan
  • Child care (if required to work)

🎯 Wants — 30%

Spending that makes life better but isn't essential.

  • Dining out, takeout and delivery
  • Streaming services
  • Gym or rec centre membership
  • Hobbies and entertainment
  • Travel and weekends away
  • Clothing beyond basics
  • Coffee shops
  • Phone or data plan upgrades
  • Concerts and hockey tickets
  • Home décor and upgrades
  • Personal care beyond basics
  • Gifts and charitable giving

💰 Savings — 20%

Money that builds security and future options.

  • Emergency fund
  • TFSA contributions
  • RRSP or group RRSP
  • FHSA (first-home buyers)
  • RESP for your kids
  • Workplace pension top-ups
  • Non-registered investing
  • Extra debt payments (above minimum)
  • Down payment savings
  • Sinking funds for big expenses

Grey Areas: Where Do These Go?

Car payment: A need if you genuinely need a vehicle to get to work and transit isn't realistic, which is common outside the big cities. The portion you pay for a nicer vehicle than you need is a want.

Prescriptions, dental and eye care: Provincial health care covers doctor visits and hospital care, but not everything. If you don't have a workplace plan, essential prescriptions and dental care are needs. Elective extras are wants.

Internet: A need for most working adults, since banking, job searches and remote work depend on it. Paying for far more speed than you use is a want.

Employer RRSP or pension matching: Always capture the full match first. It's an immediate, guaranteed return, and it counts toward your 20%.

When the Standard Split Doesn't Fit

The 50/30/20 rule is a framework, not a pass/fail test. In much of Canada, from Metro Vancouver and the Fraser Valley to the Greater Toronto Area, housing alone can push needs well past 50%. A modified split such as 60/20/20 or 70/20/10 is a legitimate starting point, as long as you protect your savings and have a plan to improve the ratio over time.

If your income changes month to month, budget from a conservative baseline and set aside taxes first. If you're carrying high-interest credit card debt, temporarily direct more than 20% toward paying it off.

Six Tips to Hit Your Targets

🏠
Tackle Housing First
Housing is the biggest needs expense and the most powerful lever on your ratios. If rent or mortgage alone takes more than about a third of your take-home pay, needs will run over 50% no matter how carefully you manage the rest. If you own, your mortgage renewal is a key moment to review your payment, amortization and lender.
⚙️
Automate Savings on Payday
Set up an automatic transfer to your TFSA, RRSP, FHSA or savings account for the day your pay arrives. Money that moves before you see it doesn't get spent. If your employer offers a group RRSP through payroll, that's an even stronger version of the same habit.
📋
Audit Subscriptions Once a Year
Set aside 30 minutes to go through every recurring charge on your credit card and bank statements. Cancel anything you haven't used in the last month. Subscription creep is one of the most common reasons wants run past 30%, and one of the easiest to fix.
📞
Re-shop Your Fixed Bills
Phone, internet and insurance are more negotiable than they feel. Compare the discount brands owned by the big carriers, call retention every year or two, and re-quote home and auto insurance at renewal. Loyalty is quietly expensive.
💳
Treat Savings Like a Bill
Pay your savings the way you pay hydro: automatically, on time, every month, without debate. Savings treated as "whatever's left over" are the first thing cut in a stressful month. Savings treated as a fixed bill keep happening.
📊
Review Once a Month
Pick a date and spend 20 minutes comparing what you actually spent with your three targets. No judgment, just information. That one habit is enough to keep most households roughly on track without daily tracking.

🤝 When to Talk to a Professional

The 50/30/20 rule is a strong starting framework. Some situations call for one-on-one advice that a calculator can't give.

Needs consistently above 60%A financial planner can look at structural fixes, such as refinancing, consolidating debt or restructuring at mortgage renewal, that a percentage target can't solve.
Choosing between RRSP, TFSA and FHSAThe right order depends on your income, tax bracket and goals. A Certified Financial Planner (CFP) can map it out for your situation.
Self-employed or gig incomeAn accountant can help you set the right CRA holdback, track deductible expenses and handle GST/HST if it applies to you.
Debt taking over your budgetFree, confidential help is available from non-profit agencies that are members of Credit Counselling Canada.
